AI Was Given 5 Puzzles Science Couldn't Solve — $1 Million If It Cracks Them

November 6, 2026 · Nodrits Solutions · 3 min read

AI was given five puzzles science couldn't solve — and $1 million if it cracks them. San Francisco startup Discovered Materials announced a million-dollar challenge on November 5, 2026, asking AI systems to solve five longstanding problems in materials science. The twist: the company already let frontier AI models try, for days, with internet access — and they made little progress.

What happened

Discovered Materials, a months-old San Francisco startup using AI models to find new substances for building advanced chips, announced the challenge on November 5, 2026, as reported by TechCrunch. The company, part of Y Combinator's spring 2026 batch, recruited an advisory group of academics at leading global universities to spell out five open problems, and is offering a pool of prizes worth $1 million in total — $200,000 per problem. The five problems: a theory to explain cuprate superconductivity, the ability of certain materials to have zero electrical resistance at ultra-low temperatures; a numerical method to predict the structures of interfaces between silicon and silicon dioxide, a material combination found in almost all semiconductors; a numerical method to map grain structure to thermal properties, enabling the design of better heat-dissipation materials; a theory to predict why shining light on materials can change their behaviour, particularly around superconductivity; and a numerical method to improve density functional theory, a key technique for modelling the structures of atoms and molecules. Stanford professor Felipe Jornada, who helped develop the challenges, told TechCrunch that understanding unresolved material behaviour could influence electronics, energy storage and manufacturing. The company previously disclosed a $9 million seed financing round in August 2026.

Why it matters

The challenge is interesting for what it admits. Discovered Materials turned frontier lab models loose on the problems first, giving AI agents internet access and other resources to work autonomously for days — and, by the company's own account, they made little progress. That is a company-led test described to TechCrunch, not an independently reproduced benchmark, but the admission itself is the point: a startup whose business is AI-discovered materials is publicly saying today's best models cannot yet do the hardest part of its job. The prize reframes the failure as a benchmark: unlike a leaderboard, these five problems have no known answer, so a solution would be a genuine scientific result, not a score. Co-founder Advaith Sridhar told TechCrunch he now expects the company to identify an industrially useful material in less than a year — an accelerated forecast, not a demonstrated achievement — and the public materials reviewed do not provide complete judging rules, deadlines or eligibility terms.

What it means for you

If you build with AI, the lesson is about honest benchmarking. A model that aces a test with known answers is not the same as a model that solves an open problem, and this challenge is a rare public admission of the gap. If you work in chips, semiconductors or energy storage, watch the five problems: a solution to any one of them — cuprate superconductivity, the silicon interface, heat dissipation — would change your supply chain, not just your software stack. If you are a researcher, note the format: $200,000 per problem, defined by academics, judged on results nobody can look up. And if you are a founder, note the marketing: a $1 million prize buys more attention than a $1 million ad campaign, and it buys it from exactly the researchers the company wants to hire.

The Nodrits take

AI has spent two years passing tests. This challenge asks it to do science. The difference matters: passing a test proves a model can find an answer that exists; solving an open problem proves it can find one that doesn't. The frontier models failed the first attempt — quietly, over days, with the whole internet at their disposal. That failure is not an embarrassment for AI. It is a measurement.
