Nvidia's Board Launches an Independent Investigation Into CEO Jensen Huang

November 8, 2026 · Nodrits Solutions · 4 min read

Nvidia's board of directors has launched an independent investigation into its own founder and chief executive, Jensen Huang, after reports that he was the target of a foreign intelligence effort. The announcement came on Sunday, November 8, alongside a message from Huang to the company in which he apologised for an affair — and the board says it has no reason to believe that US national security or Nvidia's secrets were compromised.

What happened

Nvidia said a committee of independent directors, assisted by outside independent counsel, will oversee the inquiry. The board's statement said it was acting "in light of recent reporting that founder and CEO Jensen Huang has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort," and that the review "will examine all available information."

The reports behind the move centre on Alice Huang, a former executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based company that buys Nvidia's AI processors. Bloomberg and other outlets reported that Huang sent a message to Nvidia staff admitting he had an affair with Alice Huang. A memo circulating among US policymakers earlier this year claimed Alice Huang had been recruited by China's Ministry of State Security to spy on Nvidia's chief executive. The New York Times reported in October on Huang's close relationship with the businesswoman.

In his message to the company, Jensen Huang wrote: "I owe my family and all of you an apology. I was unfaithful to my wife. My actions hurt her and our children. There is no excuse for what I did. I am deeply sorry." He added that he had "fallen short" of his responsibility to the company.

It is important to be precise about what is alleged and what is established: the espionage claims remain reported allegations, not findings. Nvidia's board statement does not name Alice Huang, Megaspeed, China or the Ministry of State Security, and it presents no evidence that company secrets were taken or that national security was harmed.

The board also said Huang "fully supports the review and has committed to cooperating with it," and expressed "confidence in Jensen and his leadership team." The company added: "Today's news will not slow NVIDIA. Jensen is committed to continuing to lead NVIDIA as CEO without interruption." Nvidia had previously said the pair's interactions were "not unusual" and that the company does not share classified information with customers.

Separately, three Republican House committee chairs — Foreign Affairs Chairman Brian Mast, Select Committee on China Chairman John Moolenaar and Intelligence Committee Chairman Rick Crawford — have asked Huang to attend a joint closed-door briefing on November 18 about Nvidia's security protocols and his interactions with the Megaspeed executive, and to respond by November 11.

Why it matters

Nvidia makes the AI chips at the centre of the US-China technology rivalry, and its chief executive is one of the most influential figures in the industry — and one with a close relationship with President Donald Trump. When the board of the world's most valuable chipmaker opens a formal probe into its own CEO, it is not a routine governance matter.

The stakes cut two ways. If the board's review finds nothing was compromised, the episode still shows how exposed even the most powerful tech leaders are to foreign intelligence operations — other companies in the AI boom have already warned staff to be wary of possible spies, including those offering sex or romance. If it finds otherwise, the fallout would reach far beyond one company: export controls, chip supply and US national-security policy all run through Nvidia.

"The Board prioritizes U.S. national and economic security, which is paramount to the interests of NVIDIA and its shareholders." — Nvidia board statement

For now, the company's message is continuity: the investigation is open, the CEO stays in place, and no conclusion has been announced.

What it means for you

For businesses building on Nvidia hardware, nothing in the announcement points to any disruption in chip supply or product roadmaps — the board's message is explicitly that the company continues as normal. The dates to watch are November 11, when Huang is due to respond to the House chairs' briefing request, and November 18, the proposed closed-door briefing.

For anyone running a company, the episode is a governance lesson: personal conduct at the top becomes a security question the moment a foreign intelligence service is alleged to be involved. Boards are now expected to act fast and in public — and Nvidia's did, within days of the latest reporting.

The Nodrits take

This story is a reminder that in the AI era, the chip supply chain is a national-security story, not just a business story. The board did the right thing by opening an independent review quickly — but the real test is whether it publishes a clear conclusion when the review ends. Until then, everything about the espionage claim is allegation, and it should be reported that way.
