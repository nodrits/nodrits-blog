Oracle Scraps $7 Billion AI Chip Deal With Tencent After US Pressure, FT Reports

November 4, 2026 · Nodrits Solutions · 4 min read

Oracle has scrapped a reported $7 billion deal to lease advanced AI chips to Tencent after pressure from the Trump administration, according to the Financial Times, as reported by Reuters on November 4, 2026. The five-year agreement would have given the Chinese tech giant access to about 100,000 advanced AI chips housed in Oracle data centers across Southeast Asia — chips that are not available in China because of export curbs from Washington and Beijing. It was, according to the FT, Tencent’s largest overseas lease deal ever — and now it is off the table.

What happened

The Financial Times reported on Wednesday, November 4, 2026, that Oracle cancelled the multibillion-dollar chip leasing contract with Tencent after US officials approached Oracle to raise concerns about the arrangement, citing people familiar with the matter. Reuters, relaying the FT report, noted that it could not immediately verify the report, and that Oracle, Tencent and the White House did not respond to its requests for comment. The original deal, first reported by the FT last month, was a five-year lease across multiple Oracle data centers in Southeast Asia, estimated at about $7 billion, with an upfront payment of roughly 30%. The report also said Tencent received visits from top officials in Guangdong province, where the company is based, who asked it to support domestic Chinese chips.

Why it matters

The cancellation shows how directly geopolitics now shapes the AI infrastructure market. Access to advanced chips is one of the most contested resources in tech: Washington has restricted China’s access to cutting-edge processors, and Beijing has pushed its own firms toward domestic alternatives. A $7 billion lease that would have routed 100,000 advanced chips to a Chinese giant through Southeast Asian data centers sat squarely in the middle of that standoff — and both governments, according to the FT report, weighed in. For cloud providers, the message is blunt: even deals structured outside China can be pulled into the US-China technology rivalry.

What it means for you

If you build on AI infrastructure, expect supply and partnership decisions to stay political for a while. Chip availability, data-center locations and vendor partnerships can change on a policy signal, not just on price or performance. Diversifying providers, keeping workloads portable, and watching export-control news as closely as pricing news is now basic risk management for any business betting on AI.

The Nodrits take

This was not a product failure or a pricing dispute — it was a deal killed by geopolitics. The question is whether this becomes the new normal for big AI infrastructure deals, or a one-off caught between two governments.
