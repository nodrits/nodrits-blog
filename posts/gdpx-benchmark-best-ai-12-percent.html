The World's Best AI Just Scored 12%: SemiAnalysis' GDPx Benchmark Resets the Hype

November 4, 2026 · Nodrits Solutions · 4 min read

The world's best AI just scored 12%. SemiAnalysis published GDPx on November 2, 2026, a benchmark built to measure what AI agents can actually do at work — and the best frontier model in the world passed barely one task in eight.

What happened

GDPx, published by SemiAnalysis analysts Max Kan, Andrew Megalaa and Dylan Patel, tests models on 206 agentic tasks across 11 professional domains chosen for their economic value: chip design, cybersecurity, biology, finance, legal, quantitative trading, recursive self-improvement, hardware design, materials science, alignment and software engineering. The tasks were built with 14 specialist data partners as reinforcement-learning environments — simulated workspaces containing the actual tools, files, APIs and context a professional would use — rather than text-only prompts. Each task carries a default time limit of eight hours, models run in their native agent harnesses (Claude Code for Opus 5.5, Codex for GPT-6 Astra, Antigravity for Gemini 4 Carbon), and scoring is pass@1 averaged over three attempts, with all domains weighted equally. The headline leaderboard, as reported by TechTimes, ExplainX and others: Claude Opus 5.5 at 12.0%, Gemini 4 Carbon at 10.9% and GPT-6 Astra at 9.6%. Under strict binary grading, every model scored zero on finance and legal tasks — the finance tasks were rebuilt from a real $880 million private-equity acquisition of a snack manufacturer across roughly 4,000 anonymised files.

Why it matters

The low ceiling is the point, and SemiAnalysis says so openly. Older benchmarks saturated as models learned to game them; vendors quoted scores near 100% while production agents kept failing on real work. GDPx is an explicit response to OpenAI's GDPval, and its design choices — binary pass/fail, eight-hour tasks, real tool environments, equal domain weighting — are all aimed at making the score hard to inflate. The cost column is the other revelation: Opus 5.5 averages $51.41 per attempt, which at a 12% pass rate means roughly $428 per successful outcome, while Gemini 4 Carbon delivers about $149 per success — though it was not generally available at publication. There are asterisks, and SemiAnalysis publishes them: Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra lose points because their safety classifiers block some cybersecurity, biology and alignment tasks, and in cyber alignment tasks 196 of 200 runs broke at least one behavioural rule. Most tasks are private, so outside groups cannot fully reproduce the results yet.

What it means for you

If you buy agents for real work, GDPx gives you a budgeting rule and a procurement question. Budget for the miss rate: on hard, realistic tasks, even the best model fails roughly seven times in eight, so keep humans in the loop and price the retries. Ask vendors for pass rates on your exact workflow — your tools, your files, your acceptance tests — not demo scores on saturated benchmarks. And read the domain breakdown, not the headline: a model that leads overall may still score zero in your domain, and the cheapest success may come from a model that is not even available to you yet.

The Nodrits take

12% is not failure. It is a map. A benchmark where the best scores 12% tells you where agents genuinely help today — chip design, hardware design, quant trading, where Opus 5.5 leads — and where they do not: finance and legal at zero is the most honest number in AI this year.
