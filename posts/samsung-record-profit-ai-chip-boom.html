Samsung Posts Record Profit as the AI Chip Boom Rewrites the Rules

October 29, 2026 · Nodrits Solutions · 3 min read

Here is the payoff: Samsung Electronics, the world's top memory chipmaker, said on Thursday that its operating profit rose nearly ninefold to a record high in the third quarter — 107.4 trillion won (about $80.04 billion), up from 12.2 trillion won a year earlier — driven by robust demand for AI chips. The result landed in line with the company's own estimate, and Samsung added that it expects the chip supply shortage to deepen next year.

What happened

Samsung reported its third-quarter results on Thursday, October 29, 2026. Operating profit hit a record 107.4 trillion won, compared with 12.2 trillion won in the same period last year. The company also said it would pay 30 trillion won in dividends for the quarter, or 4,601 won per share. The surge came from demand for the memory chips that power AI data centres, even as investors stayed cautious about how durable the AI spending boom will prove to be.

The result followed a strong report a day earlier from cross-town rival SK Hynix, which posted strong earnings and an upbeat outlook on Wednesday — a combination that helped ease market worries that AI-driven chip earnings could lose momentum, and lifted shares of both Samsung and SK Hynix.

Why it matters

Memory chips are the quiet engine of the AI boom: every AI model trained and every chatbot query served runs on hardware stuffed with them. When the world's largest memory chipmaker posts a record quarter and warns that shortages will deepen next year, it signals that AI demand is still outrunning supply — and that the chip cycle, which has swung between glut and shortage for decades, is firmly in a shortage phase.

That has knock-on effects far beyond Samsung. Tighter chip supply can raise costs for phone, PC and server makers, and it strengthens the hand of the handful of companies that can actually produce advanced memory at scale. Samsung's warning about next year suggests buyers should expect allocation battles, not price relief.

What it means for you

If you buy hardware — or you build products that run on it — expect memory pricing and availability to stay tight into next year. For businesses, that means budgeting for higher component costs and longer lead times. For everyone else, it is a reminder of how much of the AI story is really a hardware story: the models get the headlines, but the chips decide who can actually run them.

The Nodrits take

The AI boom keeps being framed as a software race, but Samsung's record quarter shows the money is still flowing hardest to the companies that make the physical foundations. The question now is whether this shortage deepens into a real bottleneck for AI growth — or whether it simply hands even more pricing power to the chip giants.
