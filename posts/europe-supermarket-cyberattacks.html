Someone Hacked the Freezers Across Europe

November 2, 2026 · Nodrits Solutions · 4 min read

Hackers did not steal data this time. They turned up the temperature. Overnight on November 2, 2026, a second wave of suspected cyberattacks hit supermarket refrigerators across Europe, forcing stores in several countries to throw out spoiled food — weeks after a similar attack struck hundreds of supermarkets in France.

What happened

Stores in Belgium, Ireland, Luxembourg, Sweden and Estonia reported sudden temperature spikes in their fridges and freezers on Monday, November 2. Dutch retail giant Ahold Delhaize confirmed an IT incident at a number of its Delhaize shops in Belgium on Sunday night, saying temperature increases in the cooling systems were noticed on Monday morning. Colruyt Group, which operates Spar stores in Belgium under a franchise system, said five of its shops had their fridge systems attacked, while Carrefour Belgium said 15 of its 700 outlets were impacted. In Ireland, Musgrave Group said a cyber incident affected refrigeration systems at 13 of its SuperValu stores. In Sweden, Coop said six of its around 800 stores had problems and had to throw out food, and one ICA store posted that its fridges and freezers had been hacked and set to 50 degrees Celsius. Sweden's national cybersecurity centre confirmed it was working on several incidents affecting grocery store cooling systems.

The method, according to Stephan Moelans, CEO of Belgian refrigeration company Sabcobel, which operates Delhaize's systems, was strikingly simple: the attackers switched the units' evaporators into defrost mode. No group has publicly claimed responsibility, and authorities have not indicated who could be behind the attacks.

Why it matters

This is the second wave in less than a month. In October, hackers seized control of cooling units at hundreds of supermarkets in France, raising temperatures as high as 50 degrees Celsius (122 degrees Fahrenheit), spoiling food and causing fires at several stores. Security researchers said the cause was internet-connected refrigerators made by Danish company Danfoss, which was no longer providing security updates — and security company Claroty had warned back in August that those units were at risk of remote hacks and outages. Ahold Delhaize had also reported a cybersecurity incident to Dutch police last month after detecting suspicious activity in its infrastructure.

The freezer hacks are a blunt reminder that cyberattacks now have physical consequences. In 2025, a cyberattack on food distributor giant UNFI resulted in food shortages and empty shelves at grocery stores across the United States. TechCrunch also reported, citing Nextgov, that US Department of Defense officials are investigating whether freezer units on military bases were hacked by Iran-backed hackers — though that link remains reported, not confirmed.

What it means for you

Every internet-connected device in a business is a door, and the ones nobody thinks about — fridges, cameras, thermostats, printers — are often the least protected. If a unit's manufacturer has stopped providing security updates, it should be replaced, isolated from the internet, or put behind a separate network that cannot reach anything else important. Temperature and system monitoring should alert a human the moment something changes, not the next morning when the food is already gone. For small businesses, the lesson is the same at a smaller scale: audit what is connected, update what can be updated, and retire what cannot.
