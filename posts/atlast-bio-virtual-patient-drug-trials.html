Atlast Bio Raises $12.5M to Replace Drug Trials With Virtual Patients

October 28, 2026 · Nodrits Solutions · 3 min read

The next drug trial has no patients. Swiss AI startup Atlast Bio has raised $12.5 million to run drug trials on virtual patients: an AI foundation model trained on about two million human RNA readouts that, the company says, can replace years of human testing — and animal testing — with 12-week simulations. Results from a first trial are expected within six weeks.

What happened

Atlast Bio, a Zurich-based AI startup, has received $12.5 million in funding led by Swedish venture capital firm Creandum, an early backer of Spotify and Trade Republic, the company said on October 27, 2026, via Reuters. The company was founded by former Google employees alongside AI and pharmaceutical specialists. Its product is a foundation model trained on about two million human RNA readouts, used to simulate how drugs behave in virtual patients. The pitch: virtual trials can evaluate drugs across multiple diseases simultaneously — something not feasible in conventional clinical trials — and can replace animal testing as well as human Phase II trials. One naming note: Reuters' own copy renders the company as both “Atlast Bio” and “Atlas Bio”; this article uses the headline spelling, Atlast Bio.

Why it matters

Phase II is where drugs go to die. The company says a Phase II trial costs more than $100 million, takes two to three years, and sees about 70% of drugs fail at that stage — years and fortunes spent learning that a drug does not work. If virtual patients can predict those failures in 12 weeks, the economics of drug development change: more candidates tested, faster, for less money, and fewer animals used. That is a large if, and the honest version of this story is that nothing is proven yet: no trial results have been published, and the company's claims rest on its own model, not on peer-reviewed evidence. The first results, due within six weeks, are the moment this story either earns its premise or loses it.

What it means for you

Watch the first results, not the pitch. When they land, the questions that matter are whether the virtual predictions matched real outcomes, and whether regulators accept virtual evidence in place of human data. Speed is the promise; safety decides adoption. For anyone outside pharma, the transferable lesson is the same one running through every AI story this week: a model is only as good as the validation it survives.

The Nodrits take

Software eats the trial. If virtual patients work, the bottleneck in medicine moves from recruiting humans to validating models — and six weeks is not long to wait for the first answer.
