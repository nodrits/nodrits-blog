OpenAI's Former Science Lead Just Launched Warp Sciences — $215M to Automate Drug Labs with Robots

October 23, 2026 · Nodrits Solutions · 3 min read

One of OpenAI's top scientific minds is done with chatbots — he wants robots running drug labs. Kevin Weil, OpenAI's former science lead, has launched Warp Sciences, a startup that trains robots to physically do wet-lab work for drug discovery, and it has already raised $215 million at an $865 million valuation, which Weil announced on X. Lightspeed led the round, with IVP, Premji Invest and Lux Capital participating.

What happened

According to TechCrunch (October 22, 2026), Warp Sciences' model is elegantly simple: researchers identify promising compounds, and robots physically make and test them. To teach the robots, the startup's human chemists wear cameras and sensor gloves while performing lab tasks — the robots learn by watching and imitating, then take over and operate around the clock.

Weil has a second motive beyond speed: geography. For years, biotech companies have outsourced the labor-intensive work of synthesizing thousands of molecule variations to contractors in China, such as WuXi AppTec. With Warp Sciences, he wants to reshore that work and insulate the entire drug development pipeline from geopolitical supply-chain risks.

Why it matters

This is big money betting that the next AI frontier isn't bigger language models — it's embodied AI doing physical science. At $215 million, this is one of the largest bets on AI-for-drug-discovery this year, and it lands as investors keep pouring billions into AI: DeepSeek reportedly raised ~$15 billion this same week, and Anthropic posted record losses while racing toward an IPO.

"Wearing cameras and sensor gloves, the startup's human chemists will train robots to perform various lab tasks… using robots that can operate around the clock." — TechCrunch

The wet lab is one of the last corners of science still running largely on human hands. If robots can learn bench work by watching chemists, the speed of drug discovery could change the way cloud computing changed software — quietly, then all at once.

What it means for you

If you work in biotech, pharma or lab services, the automation wave is officially knocking on the bench, not just the office. For founders and developers, the playbook matters: Warp isn't selling a robot — it's selling a capability trained on human expertise (cameras + sensor gloves as the data pipeline). That's a pattern worth copying: if your business has scarce expert labor, ask what a camera, a sensor and an AI model could learn from watching it. And for everyone else: drug pipelines that currently take a decade could start moving noticeably faster within this decade.

The Nodrits take

The striking part isn't the $215 million — it's the training method. Warp is treating expert human chemists as training data with hands. Every industry where skilled people do repetitive, high-value physical work is now a candidate for the same move: record the expert, train the robot, keep the expert as the teacher. Question for the audience: in your industry, which expert task would you automate first if a robot could learn it just by watching?
