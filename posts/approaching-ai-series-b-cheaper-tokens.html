Approaching.AI Raises $195 Million to Make AI Tokens Cheaper

October 27, 2026 · Nodrits Solutions · 3 min read

While the world argues about which AI model is smartest, investors just poured more than RMB 1.3 billion — about $195 million — into making AI cheaper to run. Beijing's Approaching.AI, which sells what it calls high-quality AI token production, closed its Series B on October 26, 2026, taking its total fundraising for the year to RMB 2.4 billion.

What happened

Approaching.AI (Qujing Technology), a Beijing-based AI infrastructure startup, announced a Series B financing round of more than RMB 1.3 billion (approximately $195 million) on October 26, 2026. The round was co-led by Zhejiang Finance Capital, Xiaoshan Capital, ICBC Investment, Zhongguancun Science City, Xinglian Capital and Qianjiang Century City, with SenseTime, Wangsu Science & Technology and other industrial investors participating. The company says several core operating metrics grew more than tenfold over six months, and the new funds will expand its token-production capacity and its ATaaS (Approaching.AI Token as a Service) platform.

Why it matters

Inference — the work of actually running a model after it is trained — is becoming the AI industry's biggest operating cost. Every agent call, every chatbot reply, every tool use multiplies the tokens a business pays for, and as agents chain more calls together, that bill compounds. Approaching.AI attacks it with custom ASICs and a compiler it says cuts the floating-point work per token, claiming up to 40 percent lower energy consumption than conventional GPUs, plus model quantization and dynamic batching. It is the same bet SiliconFlow and Infinigence made with their own large rounds this year: whoever serves tokens cheapest wins enterprise budgets. One honest note: the company has not disclosed revenue or per-token costs, so the round proves investor heat, not yet a proven business model.

What it means for you

Price the finished task, not the model. Track cost per 1,000 completed tasks, cache repeated answers, batch requests where latency allows, and try smaller models first for routine work. When a vendor pitches you, ask for measured latency and cost on your workload — not their fundraising headline.
