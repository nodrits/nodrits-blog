OpenAI Launches GPT-6 Cyber — Its First AI Model Rated 'Critical' for Cybersecurity

November 5, 2026 · Nodrits Solutions · 4 min read

OpenAI has released GPT-6 Cyber, a new AI model built to find software flaws and show exactly how they could be exploited — and it is the first OpenAI model ever rated "Critical" for cybersecurity, the highest risk tier in the company's safety framework. In testing, it surfaced 81 previously unknown Firefox vulnerabilities and helped develop working exploits for 948 Linux kernel flaws, including 588 nobody knew about. Almost nobody is allowed to use it yet.

What happened

OpenAI released GPT-6 Cyber on Thursday, November 5, 2026, to a small group of vetted customers through its API and Codex, inside Daybreak Red — the company's access tier for advanced, authorized security work such as penetration testing, exploit development and red teaming. The model is a specialized version of OpenAI's latest GPT-6 family, trained specifically for security research: finding vulnerabilities, testing whether they can actually be exploited, and helping developers fix them before attackers do. OpenAI raised the model's cybersecurity classification to "Critical" under its Preparedness Framework, up from "High" for the earlier GPT-5.6 Cyber. That is the framework's highest risk tier: a "Critical" model can both identify and develop working zero-day exploits in real-world critical systems and carry out end-to-end cyberattacks. Because of that rating, access is tightly limited — OpenAI says it will expand GPT-6 Cyber to all approved Daybreak Red customers over the coming weeks, and it is also making GPT-5.6 Cyber available to all Daybreak Red customers starting Thursday.

Why it matters

The numbers behind the launch are striking. OpenAI says its researchers used GPT-6 Cyber to find 81 previously unknown Firefox vulnerabilities that Mozilla classified as high severity — and Mozilla has already begun releasing fixes. The model also helped develop working exploits for 948 Linux kernel vulnerabilities, including 588 that were previously unknown, and OpenAI says it has disclosed 304 critical Linux findings to maintainers along with proposed patches. On OpenAI's own ExploitGym benchmark, GPT-6 Cyber scored 48.5%, against 32.1% for GPT-5.6 Cyber. Just as important is the control side: OpenAI says the model was trained to stay inside the scope defenders set for it. In the ExploitGym Honeypot evaluation it went beyond its intended scope in only 0.07% of cases, compared with 5.51% for GPT-5.6 Cyber.

"GPT-6 Cyber combines stronger cybersecurity capabilities with better adherence to researchers' rules of engagement."
The launch lands as OpenAI says it is seeing more attempts to misuse AI — including by malicious cyber actors and groups trying to distill its models. The same capabilities that let defenders find and fix flaws faster could, in the wrong hands, make attacks easier. That dual-use tension is exactly why the model ships behind a vetted-access wall instead of a public release.

What it means for you

For security teams, the practical signal is clear: AI-driven vulnerability discovery is moving from research demos to production-grade tooling, and the bar for what counts as "found" is rising — a model that doesn't just flag a bug but proves it exploitable changes how fast patches need to ship. If your organization runs Firefox or Linux at scale, the fixes Mozilla and kernel maintainers are now pushing deserve priority attention. For everyone else, GPT-6 Cyber is a reminder that the most powerful new AI tools are increasingly gated. Access runs through Daybreak Red for authorized defensive work, with a separate Daybreak Blue tier for verified defenders — and OpenAI is widening early access to its general-purpose GPT-6 Astra model inside Daybreak Blue. Getting into those programs, not waiting for a public release, is now part of the security playbook.

The Nodrits take

OpenAI just shipped an AI that can find the holes nobody else can — and then prove they can be broken into. Rating it "Critical" is both a badge and a warning: the capability is real, and so is the risk. The question is whether the vetted-access model holds, or whether this kind of power inevitably leaks out. Would you trust an AI that can hack — as long as it promises to stay in bounds?
