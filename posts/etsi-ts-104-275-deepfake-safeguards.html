The Deepfake Fight Just Moved Before the Video Is Made: ETSI's New Standard Explained

November 1, 2026 · Nodrits Solutions · 3 min read

The deepfake fight just moved before the video is made. ETSI released Technical Specification TS 104 275 on October 30, 2026 — a standard that treats preventing harmful deepfakes as a design and governance job, not a detection job after the damage is done.

What happened

Developed by ETSI's Technical Committee on Securing Artificial Intelligence (TC SAI), TS 104 275 builds on the baseline cybersecurity requirements for AI in ETSI's EN 304 223 and supports objectives laid out in the EU AI Act. Its core shift is scope: instead of asking how to spot a fake after it spreads, it organizes safeguards across five stages of an AI system's lifecycle — secure design, development, deployment, maintenance and end of life. The measures include risk assessments before release, documentation of risks and safeguards, content filtering, labelling of AI-generated content and user warnings, ongoing monitoring of emerging threats, investigation of incidents, updating safeguards as threats evolve, and human oversight throughout. Crucially, the specification includes test scenarios with pass/fail criteria, so organizations can demonstrate whether a reduction in deepfake-related risk has actually occurred — rather than, in ETSI's framing, relying on commitments to responsible development. Rapporteur and secretary Alex Cadzow put it plainly: AI organizations need help addressing deepfake risks much earlier, starting with system design.

Why it matters

Harmful deepfakes — abusive images, fraudulent product sites, impersonation for fraud — are increasingly made with legal, commercially available software, which means the old answer, detection, is always playing catch-up. A lifecycle standard changes the question buyers and regulators can ask: not 'do you care about deepfakes?' but 'show me your risk assessment, your filtering, your labelling, your monitoring, your test results.' It is also honest about its own limits: TS 104 275 is a voluntary technical specification, not a law, and it binds nobody unless a contract, a buyer or a regulator requires it. Its power depends on adoption — by AI developers, by the platforms that host their outputs, and by the enterprises that buy their tools and start demanding evidence.

What it means for you

If you build with generative AI, adopt the checklist now, before a customer or regulator asks for it: assess misuse risk before release, label AI-generated content clearly, filter known abuse patterns, monitor for new ones, keep records, and keep a human in the loop for high-stakes outputs. If you buy AI tools, ask vendors for exactly this evidence — their test scenarios and pass/fail results. The companies that can show their work will win procurement; the ones with only a values statement will start losing it.

The Nodrits take

Standards don't stop bad actors — but they give honest builders a yardstick, and buyers a way to tell them apart. That is how a voluntary standard becomes, over time, the floor nobody can fall below.
