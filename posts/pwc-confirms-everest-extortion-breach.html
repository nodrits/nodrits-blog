PwC Confirms Breach After Everest Extortion Group Claims 590GB Data Theft

October 24, 2026 · Nodrits Solutions · 3 min read

One of the world's Big Four accounting and consulting firms, PwC, has confirmed a cybersecurity incident after a cyber extortion group calling itself Everest claimed to have stolen 590 gigabytes of company data and posted a countdown timer, warning the data would be released if no deal is reached.

What happened

According to Reuters, PwC said on Friday that it was aware of a cybersecurity incident involving a third-party vendor used to host the firm's employee learning records. The statement came a day after Everest claimed responsibility for the breach in a message posted to its website on October 22, as tracked by cybercrime research platform eCrime.ch.

Everest's post included a countdown timer with five days remaining, along with a warning that the data would be released if a deal could not be reached. The group claims the haul includes "regional HR and learning exports, integration documentation, software components and PwC e-learning courses," and says it contains information "about individuals and their organisational and employment history," including names, email addresses, telephone numbers and addresses. PwC declined to comment on the amount of data stolen or the veracity of the group's claims.

Why it matters

A Big Four firm being publicly named in an extortion breach is significant — these firms advise the world's largest companies on security and compliance. The incident highlights how third-party vendors remain one of the weakest links in enterprise security: the compromised system wasn't PwC's own network, but an external host for employee learning records.

"The affected site does not house client data, sensitive personal data or financial information. The incident does not impact our network or any of our operations." — PwC global spokesperson, via Reuters

What it means for you

Even if you're not PwC, the pattern repeats everywhere: your vendors' security is your security. Businesses should audit which third parties hold employee or customer data, enforce breach-notification clauses in contracts, and segment systems so a compromised learning platform can't become a path into core operations. For employees at large firms, incidents like this are a reminder to be on alert for phishing that exploits leaked contact details.

The Nodrits take

Third-party breaches keep happening because vendor security reviews are treated as paperwork, not engineering. If one of the world's most scrutinized firms can be hit through a side vendor, smaller businesses need to take supply-chain security even more seriously.
