Block's Dessa: The AI That Learned to Talk Without Ever Reading a Word

October 25, 2026 · Nodrits Solutions · 3 min read

Block, Jack Dorsey's payments company, has unveiled an AI that learned to talk the way babies do: only by listening. Dessa, announced October 23, 2026, is a full-duplex speech-to-speech model trained from scratch on nothing but human conversation — no text, no books, no pretrained models — and it sounds so human that fraud watchers are already worried.

What happened

Most voice assistants today work in three steps: turn speech into text, run the text through a language model, then read the answer back as audio. Dessa skips the text entirely. "Audio in, audio out," Block's Dessa page says. Because it learned from real conversations, it knows when to cut in, when to backchannel with a "yeah" or "got it," and it hears and produces emotion, sarcasm, laughter, a frustrated sigh and a voice cracking with sadness — the things a text pipeline throws away. It is full-duplex, meaning it listens while it speaks, so interruptions and laughter land the way they do between people. Block says inference runs faster than real time, and the whole model runs locally on a MacBook Pro with an M4 Max chip or better, after training on a single Nvidia GB200 NVL72 system. Dorsey called it "the most authentic full-duplex speech model ever built" and wrote on X that "talking with it feels like talking with a person — full of emotion, nuance, pauses, and a natural cadence."

Why it matters

Dessa matters for two opposite reasons. For builders, it proves voice AI does not need the text middleman: emotion and timing survive when the model learns from sound itself, and Block says it learned language from "hundreds of times fewer words" than text models need. For everyone else, it raises the trust problem: a voice that laughs mid-sentence and notices you sound far from the mic is a voice that can fool a phone call. That is why Dessa is not public yet — it is a research preview behind a waitlist, with early access going first to a small group of AI safety researchers. Block has also published no parameter count, benchmarks, API, pricing or weights, so the evidence so far is its own demo recordings — The Verge found them mostly natural but occasionally awkward, including one where the model mistakes the speaker for someone named "Ronaldo."

What it means for you

For businesses, voice agents for support and sales are about to feel far more natural — and far harder to distinguish from humans. If you build voice products, design for emotion and timing, not just words. And if you receive sensitive calls, the new rule is simple: tone alone will no longer prove a caller is human. Verify voices on anything that moves money or data.
