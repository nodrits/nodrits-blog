One Robots.txt Line Will Soon Speak to Every AI Company: The Shared AI-Training Signal Explained

October 31, 2026 · Nodrits Solutions · 3 min read

One robots.txt line will soon speak to every AI company. On October 30, 2026, OpenAI announced it will honor a shared signal in robots.txt — the file websites use to express preferences about how their content is used — so site owners can communicate a single AI-training preference to multiple participating providers at once, instead of maintaining a separate rule for each one.

What happened

Until now, a website owner who wanted to opt out of AI training had to identify each provider's crawlers and maintain separate rules for each — GPTBot for OpenAI, and different mechanisms for every other lab. The new mechanism, defined in an individual Internet-Draft titled Machine-Readable Training Preference Signal (draft-kelley-aipref-training-preference-signal-00), authored by Kevin Kelley of Anthropic and dated October 30, 2026, introduces a single cross-vendor policy token, __usage_ai-training, that operators can attach to paths in their robots.txt groups. It changes no robots.txt grammar: the path language, longest-match precedence and group structure of the published standard, RFC 9309, are unchanged. Crucially, it separates training preference from crawl control — saying no to training is not the same as blocking crawling for search. OpenAI said it aims to implement the signal in the coming months and will share implementation details then; its existing crawler controls will continue to apply and be respected. OpenAI framed the move as drawing on the IETF's AI Preferences working group, and said it is joining other working-group participants — including Google, Microsoft and Anthropic, per the day's joint coverage — in making the announcement, encouraging other providers to honor the signal too.

Why it matters

For publishers, this is the burden falling, not disappearing. One signal beats thirty conflicting ones — if every major provider honors it, and honors it the same way. The honest caveats matter. The draft is an individual Internet-Draft: the IETF datatracker notes it is not endorsed by the IETF and has no formal standing in its standards process. The commitment is voluntary, a provider promise rather than a legal right, and the mechanism is not operating yet — OpenAI has given no firm activation date. The Wikimedia Foundation's amicus brief in the New York Times' case against OpenAI, filed the same week, is a reminder of why publishers want more than promises. Still, a shared vocabulary is how standards start: first the token, then implementations, then — if pressure holds — the expectation that everyone honors it.

What it means for you

Audit your robots.txt this week, before implementation lands. Decide your training preference per section of your site — your newsroom may choose differently from your marketing pages — and keep crawl rules and training rules in separate, documented decisions so your team can defend them. Then watch for each provider's implementation update, and test that the signal is actually respected once it goes live. A preference nobody checks is just a comment in a file.

The Nodrits take

One shared signal beats thirty conflicting ones — but only enforcement turns a signal into a right. The industry just agreed on the vocabulary; the fight now is whether honoring it becomes the norm or stays a goodwill gesture. Should AI training consent be a voluntary signal, or a law with teeth?
