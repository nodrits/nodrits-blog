AGIBOT's Worken: Robots Should Be Judged by the Work They Finish, Not How Many Ship

October 30, 2026 · Nodrits Solutions · 3 min read

Robots are no longer counted by how many are built. At Innovation Day 2026 in Shanghai on October 29, AGIBOT introduced Worken — a proposed framework for measuring the productive work robots actually perform in the real world — alongside a full stack of new hardware, models and software. The message of the day was deployment, not demos.

What happened

Worken combines the words “Work” and “Token”. It calculates what AGIBOT calls Effective Intelligent Work Hours by multiplying active robots, autonomous working hours, and an output-efficiency ratio measured against human benchmarks. Co-founder, President and CTO Peng Zhihui's argument, in the company's announcement, is that the industry has made remarkable progress in hardware, AI models and large-scale manufacturing, but now needs to answer a more practical question: “how much productive work are they actually delivering?” The event bundled five further launches: AHA3, its integrated “Three Intelligences in One” humanoid hardware platform (liquid cooling and IP67 joints, per coverage); GO-3 Preview, its next-generation embodied AI foundation model, which coverage reports ranked first on the RoboDojo and RoboTwin benchmarks; WITA-Omni 1.0, an omni-modal interaction model for natural human-robot interaction; the G2 Max industrial robot, a dual-arm machine with 21 degrees of freedom and 40 kg capacity per arm; and Link-U OS 1.0, an operating system for embodied intelligence, now being trialled by partners with a full open-source release planned by the end of 2026. AGIBOT also highlighted advances in its AGILE motion-intelligence framework, including a new self-evolving agent capability, and released the 2026 Embodied Intelligence Industry Innovation Report. Real deployments were the proof points: manufacturing work such as Longcheer's tablet production and service work such as Guangzhou Metro.

Why it matters

The metric is the message. Robot vendors have long been judged by units shipped, and AGIBOT has a strong number there too — its 20,000th robot rolled off the production line last month, and IDC reported it led global humanoid robot shipments in the first half of 2026. Worken deliberately moves the conversation past that number: a robot sitting in a warehouse is not value, and a robot working autonomously for hours, completing tasks at a measured efficiency, is. That is also why the framework needs scrutiny: a productivity metric invented by a vendor will naturally flatter that vendor's deployments, and Worken's output-efficiency ratio depends on benchmarks and human comparisons that independent testing has not yet validated. The open-source Link-U OS plan and the OpenAtom Foundation collaboration are the more durable signals — standards, not slogans, are what let buyers compare robots across vendors.

What it means for you

Whatever you automate, measure output, not headcount — or unit count. Track work completed per hour, uptime and task success, not how many robots you bought. Pilot in one real workflow before scaling, and budget for operations and maintenance, not just the machine. If a vendor offers you its own productivity metric, ask for the benchmark, the baseline and an independent test.

The Nodrits take

Shipments flatter vendors; Worken flatters deployments. But a vendor's own metric needs independent testing before buyers should trust it. Should robots be judged by the work they finish — and who should set that standard, vendors or buyers? Tell us in the comments on today's post.
