The Browser That Blocked Ads Is Now in Court: Publishers Sue Brave Over AI Scraping

November 3, 2026 · Nodrits Solutions · 3 min read

The browser that blocked ads is now in court. The New York Times sued Brave Software on November 2, 2026, alleging the privacy-focused browser company scraped its copyrighted content and sold it on to AI companies — and three more major publishers filed a second suit the same day.

What happened

Both suits were filed in federal court in Manhattan, according to TechCrunch's November 2 report. The Times' complaint alleges Brave built an index of more than 30 billion web pages using an unidentified crawler that publishers cannot detect or block, then sold AI companies paid access to real-time content from that index for training models and answering user queries. The filing names Anthropic, Perplexity, Mistral, xAI and Cohere as customers of that access; they are not defendants in the suits. Separately, the companies operating the New York Daily News, the Chicago Tribune and the Orlando Sentinel filed a similar suit the same day. The complaints also target a Brave browser feature described as a Paywall blocker, which the filings say makes subscription content accessible without a login, and Brave's practice of blocking publishers' ads while selling its own. The Times, which has previously sued OpenAI and Perplexity over similar claims, calls Brave a supplier, dealer and distributor of stolen copyrighted content, and seeks an injunction and damages. Brave had not responded to requests for comment at the time of reporting, and no court has ruled on the merits — everything above is allegation, not finding.

Why it matters

This is a new front in the AI copyright war, and the choice of target is the point. Earlier publisher suits went after the model builders — OpenAI, Perplexity — whose answers reproduce the content. This one goes after the middleman: the index that collects, organises and sells the web's words to many AI companies at once. If a court treats selling index access as distributing copied content, the ruling would reach beyond Brave into every search and data pipeline that feeds AI systems. If it does not, publishers lose one of their few levers over a supply chain that currently pays them nothing for the raw material. Either way, the case will shape what the web's content is worth, and who gets paid for it.

What it means for you

For publishers and creators, the case is a reminder to know where your content flows after publication — which crawlers you can block, which you cannot, and what your terms actually say. For businesses building on AI, it is a supply-chain warning: ask your vendors where their training and retrieval data comes from, because the legal exposure is moving upstream to the data suppliers. And for readers, the stakes are practical: the outcome could change what stays freely searchable, what sits behind harder paywalls, and whether the AI answers you rely on keep citing the journalism that produced them.

The AI copyright fight just moved upstream. The index is now the defendant. Should a browser's search index be allowed to sell publishers' content to AI companies — or is that theft, even if the pages were public? Tell us in the comments on today's post.
